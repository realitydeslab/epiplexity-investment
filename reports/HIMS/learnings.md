## What This Tells Us About the Market

### 1. Pharma is Embracing DTC Distribution
The Novo Nordisk-HIMS deal signals a fundamental shift: Big Pharma is willing to partner with DTC telehealth platforms rather than fight them. This is analogous to when traditional media companies started licensing content to Netflix instead of competing. Implications:
- Every major pharma company will evaluate DTC telehealth partnerships
- The telehealth sector may see a wave of pharma distribution deals
- Compounding pharmacies are the big losers — regulatory enforcement + branded alternatives

### 2. The GLP-1 Economy is Massive and Expanding
The global GLP-1 market is projected at $100B+ by 2030. Distribution is the bottleneck — not demand. Companies that solve GLP-1 access (convenience, cost, compliance) will capture outsized value. HIMS is positioned as a primary access point.

### 3. Short Squeezes Create Entry Opportunities
HIMS went from $70 → $14 → $22 in 4 months. The shorts were right about the risk but wrong about the resolution. The Novo deal is a lesson in how binary catalysts can violently reverse short theses.

## Contrarian Angle — What's the Market Missing?

### The Bull Contrarian Case:
The market still prices HIMS at 2.15x sales — a "risky telehealth company" multiple. But after the Novo deal, HIMS is actually a **branded pharmaceutical distribution platform with a subscription model**. That deserves 4-6x sales (= $40-60/share). The market hasn't re-rated HIMS yet because the deal just happened today.

### The Bear Contrarian Case:
The Novo deal may actually HURT margins. Compounded semaglutide had ~80% gross margins. Branded Wegovy/Ozempic distribution may have 30-40% margins. Revenue goes up, but profits may not. If HIMS' blended gross margin falls below 65%, the profitability story unravels.

### The Hidden Risk:
HIMS took on $972M in debt for Eucalyptus. With interest rates still elevated, that's ~$50-70M/year in interest expense. Combined with lower margins on branded drugs, HIMS could become a high-revenue, low-profit company — the opposite of what investors want.
